## Introduction

Baikonur is one of the few places on Earth where a strange light in the sky is more likely to have been made by humans than by anything mysterious. The launch site in the Kazakh steppe has produced some of the most memorable “UFO-like” sightings in the region: glowing spirals, expanding clouds, moving lights and unusual colours that appear suddenly after sunset or before dawn. These events are usually not evidence of unknown craft, but the result of rockets, fuel releases and sunlight interacting with the upper atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Baikonur_Cosmodrome" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esa.int">[European Space Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esa.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">European Space Agency ESA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">European Space AgencyESA - Baikonur CosmodromeDecember 31, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 2003</span></span></span>

That does not make the stories uninteresting. Baikonur occupies a rare position in [Kazakhstan]({{ 'why-does-kazakhstan-feel-so-uncanny/' | relative_url }})’s strange-sky history because it combines genuine technological spectacle with Cold War secrecy. A person seeing a luminous shape above an empty steppe, with little public information about what had launched or why, was encountering something real but difficult to identify. The result was a perfect environment for UFO reports: unusual sights, limited explanations and a landscape already associated with hidden military activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S009457651831751X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect From Tyuratam Missile Range to Baikonur Cosmodrome</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">From Tyuratam Missile Range to Baikonur Cosmodrome - ScienceDirectFebruary 1, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2019</span></span></span>

## Sputnik, Soyuz and the spaceport on the steppe

Baikonur’s role in creating unusual sky phenomena begins with its role in creating the modern space age itself. Built by the Soviet Union in the 1950s as a secret missile facility, the site launched Sputnik 1 in 1957 and carried Yuri Gagarin into orbit in 1961. The European Space Agency notes that the cosmodrome became the launch point for Soviet crewed missions, planetary probes and many satellite programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Baikonur_Cosmodrome" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esa.int">[European Space Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esa.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">European Space Agency ESA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">European Space AgencyESA - Baikonur CosmodromeDecember 31, 2003...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 2003</span></span></span> NASA likewise identifies Sputnik’s launch from Baikonur as the event that opened the space age and intensified the Cold War space race.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/oct-4-1957-sputnik-dawn-of-space-age/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">oct 4 1957 sputnik dawn of space age</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oct. 4, 1957 - Sputnik, the Dawn of the Space Age - NASAOctober 4, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 4, 2017</span></span></span>

For ordinary observers in Kazakhstan, however, space technology did not always look like technology. Rockets were often seen from great distances, sometimes far from the launch site itself. A resident of the steppe might see a bright object moving silently or an enormous glowing formation spreading across the heavens. Before widespread internet access, satellite tracking apps and public launch schedules, identifying such events was much harder.

The name itself added to the atmosphere of mystery. During the Soviet period, officials deliberately used the name and coordinates of a different place, the town of Baikonur, to obscure the true location of the launch complex. The actual facility was hundreds of kilometres away near Tyuratam. This secrecy was part of Cold War security, but it also helped create an information gap around unusual sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S009457651831751X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect From Tyuratam Missile Range to Baikonur Cosmodrome</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">From Tyuratam Missile Range to Baikonur Cosmodrome - ScienceDirectFebruary 1, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2019</span></span></span>

## Rocket plumes, spirals and misidentified sky lights

The most famous Baikonur-related “UFO” effect is the glowing rocket plume. A rocket climbing high into the atmosphere can release exhaust that expands dramatically as surrounding air pressure falls. At high altitude, the exhaust may still be illuminated by sunlight even though observers on the ground are already in darkness. The result can be a huge glowing shape that looks nothing like a normal aircraft trail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://philsa.gov.ph/news/advisory-on-space-jellyfish-sighting-on-11-april-2026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: philsa.gov.ph">[Philippine Space Agency (PhilSA)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">philsa.gov.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Philippine Space Agency (Phil SA)Philippine Space Agency</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Philippine Space Agency (PhilSA)Philippine Space AgencyApril 12, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 12, 2026</span></span></span>

These displays are sometimes called “space jellyfish” because the plume can resemble a bright head with trailing strands. Photographs taken after Soyuz launches from Baikonur have captured these effects, including a widely seen 2019 Soyuz MS-13 plume photographed from Kazakhstan. The appearance came from sunlight reflecting from expanding exhaust gases rather than an unknown object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ALaunch_of_the_Soyuz_MS-13_launch_vehicle.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Launch of the Soyuz MS-13 launch vehicle.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Launch of the Soyuz MS-13 launch vehicle.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJuly 20, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 20, 2019</span></span></span>

Rocket launches can also create spiral patterns. When a rocket stage releases gases while rotating or changing trajectory, the expanding cloud can form a geometric swirl. To a viewer without the launch context, the shape can appear artificial in a different sense: too symmetrical, too large and too unlike ordinary weather. Similar spiral sightings elsewhere have been traced to rocket exhaust illuminated at high altitude, showing why the same visual confusion repeatedly appears around space-launch nations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/ufo-lights-siberia-russia-missile-tests-spd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicMysterious Balls of Light Aren&#x27;t UFOs, Says Science &#124; National GeographicOctober 27, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2017</span></span></span>

The key point is that these sightings are not “fake UFOs”. The witnesses are often seeing something genuinely unusual. The mistake lies in interpreting an unfamiliar technological event as an unknown craft. Baikonur is unusual because the source of the mystery is not hidden from science; it is hidden from the observer’s immediate view.

## Cold War secrecy and the mood of suspicion

Technology alone does not explain why Baikonur became linked with UFO reports. The Cold War atmosphere mattered. The Soviet Union’s space and missile programmes were surrounded by secrecy, and many details of launches, tests and failures were unavailable to the public. A strange light over a restricted military landscape naturally encouraged speculation.

One example from the wider Soviet missile environment shows how this atmosphere shaped reports. A declassified CIA document described a 1973 sighting at a Soviet missile-related site in present-day Kazakhstan involving a bright green circular object. The report records the observation as an unidentified sighting, but it does not establish what the object was. Such documents are valuable because they show the existence of reports and official interest, not proof of extraterrestrial activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exonews.org/declassified-ufo-report-documents-green-circular-object-over-soviet-missile-range/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exonews.org">[Exo News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exonews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exo News Declassified UFO Report Documents ‘Green Circular Object’ Over Soviet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exo News Declassified UFO Report Documents ‘Green Circular Object’ Over Soviet</span></span></span>

For UFO enthusiasts, cases like this remain intriguing because they sit beside genuine secret aerospace activity. For sceptics, the same circumstances provide an alternative explanation: military areas generate unusual sightings precisely because observers have incomplete information about aircraft, missiles and experiments.

Kazakhstan’s geography strengthens this effect. The vast steppe offers long horizons, dark skies and few nearby landmarks. A bright object moving above a flat landscape can seem enormous and close because there are fewer familiar reference points. The same emptiness that makes the steppe beautiful also makes it an excellent stage for optical uncertainty.

## Why Baikonur still produces UFO stories

Baikonur’s strange-sky reputation survives because it represents a collision between ancient human curiosity about the heavens and modern human engineering. People have always looked upward and searched for meaning in unusual lights. In the Baikonur era, the sky became a place where rockets, satellites and military technology created spectacles that previous generations could never have imagined.

The enduring mystery is therefore not whether rockets are mistaken for UFOs. That mechanism is now well understood. The more interesting question is why these sightings remain memorable. The answer lies in the combination of isolation, secrecy, technological wonder and the emotional impact of seeing something extraordinary without knowing its cause.

Baikonur’s lights are a modern form of sky folklore: stories built around real events, shaped by uncertainty and preserved because the experience of witnessing them feels genuinely strange. The explanation may be a rocket plume, but the human reaction — surprise, curiosity and a search for meaning — is much older than the space age itself.
