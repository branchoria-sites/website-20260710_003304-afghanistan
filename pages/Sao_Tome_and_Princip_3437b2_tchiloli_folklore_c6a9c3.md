## Introduction

Tchiloli is one of the most unusual cultural traditions in So Tom and Prncipe: a centuries-old open-air theatre where a European medieval tragedy has been transformed into a living island performance about justice, power, death and moral order. It is not a supernatural event in the sense of a documented paranormal case, but it belongs to the same cultural landscape as local stories about spirits, ancestors, rituals and unseen forces. Its strangeness comes from the way a 16th-century European drama became a Santomean tradition with its own symbolism, music, social meaning and ritual atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/tchiloli-living-theatre-of-sao-tome-and-principe-in-the-quest-of-justice-02309?RL=02309" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[UNESCO ICH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ICHTchiloli, living theatre of Sao Tome and Principe in the quest of justice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO ICHTchiloli, living theatre of Sao Tome and Principe in the quest of justice - UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

For readers interested in strange histories and living folklore, Tchiloli matters because it shows how communities preserve ideas about the unseen world without separating them neatly from everyday life. The theatre, traditional beliefs and oral storytelling of the islands are not evidence that supernatural forces exist, but they reveal how people use stories, performances and rituals to explore justice, memory, danger and the relationship between human beings and forces beyond ordinary experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsaotomeprincipe.st/en/node/55" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsaotomeprincipe.st">[Visit So Tom and Prncipe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsaotomeprincipe.st</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit So Tom and Prncipe Croyances &#124; So Tom E Prncipe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit So Tom and Prncipe Croyances &#124; So Tom E Prncipe</span></span></span>

## The Tchiloli performance tradition

### A medieval tragedy reborn on an island

The story performed in Tchiloli comes from *The Tragedy of the Marquis of Mantua and the Emperor Charlemagne*, a drama linked to the Portuguese playwright Baltasar Dias in the 16th century. The plot centres on a murder: Dom Carloto, son of Charlemagne, kills Valdevinos, leading to a conflict over justice and whether even a powerful rulers family must answer for wrongdoing. Over time, the story travelled to So Tom, where performers reshaped it into one of the archipelagos most distinctive cultural expressions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tchiloli.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tchiloli.com">[TCHILOLI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tchiloli.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HISTRIA &#124; TCHILOLIMay 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2012</span></span></span>

The result is not simply a preserved European play. Tchiloli became a local institution performed by community groups, traditionally known as *tragdias*, with actors inheriting roles, costumes and knowledge through generations. A performance may involve around thirty participants, music, dance, speeches and audience interaction. The open-air setting, often in village spaces during festivals, gives the event a communal quality closer to a public ritual than a conventional theatre performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tchiloli.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tchiloli.com">[TCHILOLI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tchiloli.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HISTRIA &#124; TCHILOLIMay 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2012</span></span></span>

One reason Tchiloli feels unusual to outsiders is the collision of different cultural worlds. European court imagery, kings, judges and legal arguments appear alongside African performance practices, local music and communal participation. The result is a form of cultural blending sometimes described by researchers as a creolised or syncretic theatre: a tradition created through the meeting and transformation of different influences rather than a simple survival of one original form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tchiloli.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tchiloli.com">[TCHILOLI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tchiloli.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HISTRIA &#124; TCHILOLIMay 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2012</span></span></span>

### Why a murder trial became a powerful island story

The supernatural atmosphere surrounding Tchiloli does not come from ghosts appearing on stage. Instead, it comes from the ritual seriousness of the performance and the way the drama deals with questions that often appear in folklore: what happens when justice fails, how communities respond to violence, and whether powerful people can escape consequences.

The central conflict is especially meaningful because the accused is a prince. The story asks whether status should protect someone from judgement. In this sense, Tchiloli functions as a symbolic court where communities repeatedly revisit ideas of fairness and responsibility. UNESCOs description of the tradition highlights this focus on a murder trial and the principle that justice should apply even to those with high social status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/tchiloli-living-theatre-of-sao-tome-and-principe-in-the-quest-of-justice-02309?RL=02309" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[UNESCO ICH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ICHTchiloli, living theatre of Sao Tome and Principe in the quest of justice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO ICHTchiloli, living theatre of Sao Tome and Principe in the quest of justice - UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

The long duration of performances also changes how audiences experience the story. Rather than consuming a short spectacle, communities participate in a shared event involving music, commentary and repeated engagement with familiar characters. The tradition survives partly because it is not only a story being told; it is a social occasion where identity and collective memory are renewed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tchiloli.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tchiloli.com">[TCHILOLI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tchiloli.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HISTRIA &#124; TCHILOLIMay 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2012</span></span></span>

## Spirits, rituals and oral storytelling

### The unseen world in island traditions

So Tom and Prncipe has a long tradition of beliefs involving ancestors, spirits and forces connected with particular places. These ideas developed alongside Christianity and the cultural experiences of African communities, Portuguese colonisation and plantation society. Traditional beliefs recorded by cultural sources describe spirits associated with nature, ancestors and protective or harmful forces, as well as practices involving prayers, symbolic objects and traditional healing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsaotomeprincipe.st/en/node/55" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsaotomeprincipe.st">[Visit So Tom and Prncipe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsaotomeprincipe.st</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit So Tom and Prncipe Croyances &#124; So Tom E Prncipe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit So Tom and Prncipe Croyances &#124; So Tom E Prncipe</span></span></span>

Within this environment, performances such as Tchiloli can take on meanings beyond entertainment. The theatre does not claim to summon spirits or prove supernatural events, but its combination of music, costume, repetition and community participation gives it a ceremonial quality. For many societies around the world, storytelling traditions often operate in this space between history, morality and belief: they preserve lessons about the unseen without functioning as scientific explanations of reality.

Other island traditions show a similar relationship between performance and mystery. The island of Prncipe, for example, has the *Auto de Floripes*, another popular theatre tradition connected with religious and mythical themes. Like Tchiloli, it demonstrates how inherited stories can remain active parts of community life rather than becoming museum pieces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tchiloli.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tchiloli.com">[TCHILOLI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tchiloli.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HISTRIA &#124; TCHILOLIMay 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2012</span></span></span>

### Oral memory and the survival of strange stories

Folklore survives because it adapts. Stories about spirits, warnings, unusual beings or sacred places can change as they move between generations, but the underlying purpose often remains: explaining uncertainty, reinforcing social values and connecting present communities with the past.

This is where So Tom and Prncipes supernatural traditions fit into a wider Fortean interest in strange histories. The important question is not whether every supernatural story can be proven. Instead, these traditions show how human societies preserve encounters with mystery. A tale about an ancestors presence, a forest spirit or a symbolic ritual may reveal fears, hopes and historical memories even when there is no physical evidence for the supernatural claim itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsaotomeprincipe.st/en/node/55" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsaotomeprincipe.st">[Visit So Tom and Prncipe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsaotomeprincipe.st</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit So Tom and Prncipe Croyances &#124; So Tom E Prncipe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit So Tom and Prncipe Croyances &#124; So Tom E Prncipe</span></span></span>

Tchiloli is especially valuable because it provides a rare recorded example of this process. Unlike many oral traditions that survive mainly through memory, it remains visible through performances, research and cultural documentation. Its survival allows researchers and audiences to see how a community keeps a centuries-old story alive while continuing to reinterpret it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/tchiloli-living-theatre-of-sao-tome-and-principe-in-the-quest-of-justice-02309?RL=02309" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ich.unesco.org">[UNESCO ICH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ich.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ICHTchiloli, living theatre of Sao Tome and Principe in the quest of justice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO ICHTchiloli, living theatre of Sao Tome and Principe in the quest of justice - UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage...</span></span></span>

## Folklore versus evidence

Tchiloli and So Toms supernatural traditions occupy an interesting middle ground between history and mystery. There is strong evidence that Tchiloli exists as a performance tradition, that its story has historical roots, and that it continues to be practised by communities in the islands. There is much less evidence for treating associated spiritual beliefs as literal supernatural events.

This distinction does not make the traditions less important. Folklore is not simply a collection of claims waiting to be proven or disproven. It is also a record of how people understand justice, suffering, nature, death and the unknown. A belief in spirits may reflect a communitys moral framework or relationship with its environment rather than a testable claim about physical reality.

The lasting appeal of Tchiloli comes from this mixture of certainty and mystery. The play itself is real, the performances are documented, and the themes are clear. What remains open to interpretation is the deeper meaning carried by the rituals, symbols and atmosphere surrounding it.
